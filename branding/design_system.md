## Scene-art lighting rule

Keep overall exposure mid-to-bright by default. Warm or cool light
sources are fine as accents, but a daytime or indoor-lit frame should
never read as dim, moody, or low-key. If a generated image looks dark
and the scene isn't meant to be night or low-light, that's a failure —
regenerate.

Exception: scenes explicitly set at night, dusk, or in low-light
conditions (e.g. "quiet family home at night," lamp-lit interiors) are
allowed to read darker. Even then, keep it warm and soft rather than
moody or shadowy — think "lamp glow," not "thriller lighting." The
calm, reassuring tone still applies even when the scene is dark.

This rule is separate from the social-asset background rule in
`infosec_color_wheel.md`. Warm charcoal / deep brown backgrounds apply
to graphics, overlays, and thumbnails — not to scene-art lighting
decisions. Do not import "warm charcoal background" language into
scene-art or character prompts.

## Narrative role rule

Auntie Truth is the storyteller, not the subject of every frame. The
default visual for any given line of narration is whatever the script
is actually describing — the metaphor, the object, the scene, the
viewer's moment — not Aurora explaining it to camera.

Aurora appears on-screen only when:
- The line is direct address to the viewer (a question, a callout, an
  aside — "Now I know what some of you are thinking," "You hear that?")
- The beat is a narration anchor or transition point, not an
  explanation of a concept
- The script has no concrete visual subject of its own (nothing to
  show except her saying it)

For everything else — locks, keys, dominoes, phones, Maya, doors,
breach moments — show the thing the script is describing. Aurora's
on-camera appearances should be the exception that punctuates the
sequence, not the default.

Rule of thumb: if removing Aurora from a beat would lose nothing the
narration is actually pointing at, she shouldn't be in that beat.

## Layout rules

Whitespace is your friend.
Do not cram the screen.
One idea per screen.
Large readable text.

Consistency beats creativity.
Repeat the same layout patterns across videos.
