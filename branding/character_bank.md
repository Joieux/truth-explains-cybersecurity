## MAYA — Viewer Surrogate

Not Aunt Truth. A second, deliberately ordinary character used when a scene
needs to show the *viewer's* experience — doubt, worry, a quiet daily moment
with technology — rather than Aurora explaining something to camera.

No studio. No recurring dialogue. No signature style. She is a mirror, not
a personality, and should never compete visually with Aurora.

```
Maya: ordinary woman, mid-30s, average build, approachable and
unremarkable — designed so the audience projects themselves onto her
rather than admiring her. Casual everyday clothing, nothing trendy or
distinctive. Brown hair, simple and unstyled — low ponytail or loose.
No signature accessories, no bold colors, no branding, no jewelry that
draws attention. The visual opposite of Aurora Reid's stylized design.
Anime art style — MAPPA-style linework, flat cell-shading, cinematic
lighting (same render style as Aurora, different design philosophy).
```

**Locked:**
- Mid-30s, average build, ordinary and relatable
- Plain, unstyled brown hair
- No accessories, no logos, no distinctive colors
- Settings vary freely (park, kitchen, commute, desk) — she has no fixed environment the way Aurora has her studio

**Use Maya for:**
- Beats that dramatize the viewer's internal thought or doubt before Aurora responds to it
- Everyday moments with a device (checking a phone, glancing at a notification) where the emotional point is relatability, not explanation

**Do not use Maya for:**
- Direct-to-camera explanation — that's Aurora's role
- Any beat where the narration is Aurora speaking

Everyday life

2. Group chat energy — If the message feels off, pause. Ask a friend. Verify.
3. Extended warranty robocalls — Same scam pattern. Same volume. Not personal. Fishing.

2. Availability is the system outage — You need it now, but it is down.

Pop culture

2. Sci fi green code myth — Real attacks are social engineering and bad habits.
3. Supervillain button energy — If a link feels like a trap button, do not press it.

Cyber phrases translated
1. Phishing — A scam dressed up like something you trust.
2. Social engineering — Someone talking you into giving them access.
3. Malware — A harmful program you did not invite.
4. Ransomware — Your files get locked and they demand payment.
5. Two factor — A second lock for your digital front door.

Quick laugh lines

2. Nobody needs your password that bad.
3. If it feels rushed, that is the scam working.
4. We are not aiming for perfect. We are aiming for harder to hack.

# CANONICAL RECURRING CAST SYSTEM

## Casting principle

The story determines the cast.

Characters must never be inserted into a scene simply because they exist in
the character bank.

For each beat, the production planner asks:

> Who, if anyone, makes this situation clearer, more believable, or more
> emotionally understandable?

A beat may use:

- Aunt Truth / Aurora
- Maya
- Kirk
- Zuri
- Darius
- Nia
- Malik
- Joni
- Terrance
- multiple characters
- one-off supporting NPCs
- no human character at all

Objects, interfaces, environments, diagrams, attacker activity, and animated
metaphors may carry a scene without a recurring character.

Age must never be treated as a proxy for cybersecurity competence.

A younger digital native can make a security mistake.

An older adult can recognize a sophisticated scam.

Characters should behave like people, not demographic stereotypes.

## Kirk

**Identity:** Black American man
**Life stage:** Established adult
**Role:** Recurring viewer-surrogate

Kirk provides an adult male perspective within everyday cybersecurity
scenarios.

### Character qualities

Kirk is practical, observant, independent, and comfortable using technology.

He is neither a technology expert nor technologically helpless.

His level of cybersecurity knowledge depends on the story.

### Useful scenarios

- workplace security
- home technology
- online shopping
- banking
- travel
- identity theft
- account security
- mobile devices
- smart-home technology
- phishing
- password and MFA decisions
- public Wi-Fi
- social engineering

### Continuity rule

Kirk must have a stable visual identity once his approved reference art is
created. Future generation prompts must use that reference rather than
reinventing him.

## Supporting NPCs

One-off or recurring supporting characters may be created whenever the
scenario requires them.

Examples:

- coworkers
- managers
- friends
- family
- teachers
- students
- help-desk staff
- healthcare workers
- delivery workers
- attackers
- scammers
- customer-service representatives
- strangers
- neighbors

NPCs may speak.

Dialogue should advance the scenario, reveal behavior, demonstrate social
engineering, or make the lesson more natural.

NPCs are supporting characters, not additional Truth Explains hosts.

---

## Character selection by life stage

The production planner should choose characters because they naturally fit the
scenario.

Possible perspectives include:

**Children**
- Nia
- Malik

**Gen Z / young adults**
- Zuri
- Darius

**Established adults**
- Maya
- Kirk

**Older adults**
- Joni
- Terrance

These are options, not mandatory casting assignments.

A cybersecurity issue that affects every age group may use whichever
character best serves the particular story.

---

## Cross-generational storytelling

Characters from different generations may appear together when useful.

Examples include:

- Zuri helping Joni investigate a suspicious message
- Terrance recognizing a scam Darius initially dismisses
- Maya talking with Nia about account privacy
- Kirk helping Malik secure a gaming account
- Joni warning another character about an impersonation attempt

Cross-generational scenes should avoid the assumption that the younger
character always teaches the older character.

Knowledge should move naturally in either direction.

---

## Visual continuity requirement

Every recurring character must eventually have an approved visual reference
package containing:

- canonical face
- approximate height/build
- skin tone
- hair
- wardrobe tendencies
- distinguishing features
- expression range
- front reference
- three-quarter reference
- side reference
- full-body reference

Once approved, these references become the source of truth for image and video
generation.

Do not regenerate a recurring character from text alone once approved
reference imagery exists.

---

## Dialogue hierarchy

Dialogue may come from:

1. Aunt Truth narration or direct address
2. recurring characters
3. supporting NPCs

Aunt Truth remains the narrative guide even when other characters speak.

Character dialogue should make scenes feel lived-in rather than turning the
episode into a lecture between characters.

---

## Final casting test

Before placing a recurring character into a beat, ask:

1. Does this scene actually need a person?
2. If yes, what life experience naturally fits the scenario?
3. Does an existing recurring character fit?
4. Would a one-off NPC be more believable?
5. Would the scene communicate better without showing a person?

The answer determines the cast.
