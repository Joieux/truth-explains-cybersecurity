### Beat 02 — Password-as-key metaphor

**Narration:**

> Picture this.  
>
> Somebody got your password.  
> Maybe from a breach.  
> Maybe from a list.  
> Doesn't matter how.  
>
> They're standing at your front door,  
> holding a *copy of your key.*  
>
> They slide it in.  
> They turn it.  
>
> And the door —  
> does not —  
> open.  
>
> Because you put a deadbolt on that door.  
> And the deadbolt doesn't take keys.  
> It takes *you.*  
>
> That is two factor authentication.  
> And it turns a thief with your key  
> into a person standing in a hallway.

**Required meaning:**

A stolen password is analogous to a copied key. Two-factor authentication adds a second lock that prevents a thief from entering with the password alone.

### Beat 03 — The two factors

**Narration:**

> Here's how it works.  
>
> To get in, you need two different things.  
>
> Something you *know* —  
> that's the password.  
>
> Something you *have* —  
> your phone, an app, a little hardware key.  
>
> Or something you *are* —  
> your face, your fingerprint.  
>
> One gets stolen?  
> The other one holds the door.

**Required meaning:**

Two-factor authentication combines a knowledge factor with a possession factor or biometric factor, so one compromised factor alone is insufficient.

### Beat 04 — Limits of SMS codes

**Narration:**

> Now — real talk.  
> Because I will never sell you a fairy tale.  
>
> Not all second locks are equal.  
>
> Those codes that come by text message?  
> Better than nothing.  
> But there's a trick called a SIM swap —  
> where a scammer sweet-talks your phone company  
> into moving YOUR number  
> onto THEIR phone.  
>
> Now your text codes  
> are knocking on their door.

**Required meaning:**

SMS one-time codes are helpful but can be compromised through a SIM-swap attack in which a criminal transfers the victim’s phone number to another device.
