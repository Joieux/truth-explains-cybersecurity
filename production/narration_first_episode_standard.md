# Truth Explains — Narration-First Episode Standard

Status: Canonical
Series: Truth Explains Cybersecurity
Narrator: Aunt Truth / Aurora Reid

## Purpose

Truth Explains is a narrated visual cybersecurity series.

Aunt Truth is the storyteller.

She is not the visual subject of every scene.

The narration explains the idea while animation, objects, environments,
metaphors, viewer-surrogate moments, sound design, and music carry the story.

The episode should feel like Aunt Truth is telling you a story while the world
of that story unfolds on screen.

---

## Core rule

For every beat, ask:

> What should the viewer SEE while Aunt Truth says this?

Do not default to:

> Show Aunt Truth saying this.

If the narration describes a phone, password, door, scam message, key,
attacker, mistake, reaction, consequence, or everyday situation, show that
thing.

Aunt Truth appears only when her presence adds meaning.

---

## Aunt Truth's role

Aunt Truth is:

- narrator
- storyteller
- trusted guide
- occasional direct-address host
- transition anchor
- emotional grounding presence

Aunt Truth is NOT:

- a talking head for every paragraph
- required in every beat
- the protagonist of every example
- a substitute for visual storytelling

### Use Aunt Truth on screen when

- opening or closing the episode
- speaking directly to the viewer
- making an especially important point
- transitioning between major ideas
- introducing or resolving a metaphor
- the narration has no better concrete visual subject

### Keep Aunt Truth off screen when

- the narration describes a specific object
- the narration describes an action
- the narration describes another person's experience
- the viewer should be watching a consequence unfold
- an animated metaphor communicates the idea better
- Maya or another viewer-surrogate moment is more natural

Rule of thumb:

> If removing Aunt Truth from the frame loses nothing the narration is pointing
> at, she should probably not be in that beat.

---

## Supporting characters / NPCs

Truth Explains may use recurring or one-off supporting characters whenever the
story benefits from showing another person in the situation.

NPCs exist to make the cybersecurity scenario concrete and believable.

Examples include:

- coworkers
- friends
- family members
- managers
- attackers or scammers
- help-desk staff
- delivery drivers
- strangers on public Wi-Fi
- customers
- employees
- neighbors
- other ordinary technology users

NPCs may speak when dialogue makes the scene clearer, more natural, or more
memorable.

They are not additional hosts.

Their purpose is to:

- create believable situations
- demonstrate social engineering
- show different reactions or decisions
- provide dialogue that advances the lesson
- represent roles needed by the scenario
- make the world feel populated rather than staged

NPC dialogue should remain concise and subordinate to the episode's teaching
goal.

Recurring NPCs may develop recognizable traits over time, but they must not
compete with Aunt Truth's role as narrator and guide.

Use an NPC when the scene logically needs another human being.

Do not force Maya into every ordinary-person role simply because she already
exists.

---

## Maya's role

Maya is the primary viewer surrogate.

She represents ordinary people experiencing technology, uncertainty,
mistakes, scams, security decisions, and everyday digital life.

Maya is not a second host.

She should not compete with Aunt Truth for personality or brand identity.

Use Maya for:

- receiving suspicious messages
- reacting to security prompts
- forgetting or reusing passwords
- navigating confusing technology
- making ordinary user decisions
- demonstrating the viewer's point of view

Maya should remain visually relatable and understated.

---

## Episode storytelling structure

Each episode should generally move through these stages:

### 1. Hook

Show the problem before explaining it.

Create curiosity, recognition, tension, or surprise.

### 2. Everyday reality

Ground the cybersecurity concept in something familiar.

Show what happens to ordinary people.

### 3. Explanation

Aunt Truth explains what is actually happening.

Visuals should demonstrate the mechanism rather than merely decorate the
narration.

### 4. Consequence

Show why the issue matters.

The viewer should understand what changes when the security control fails.

### 5. Practical action

Show exactly what the viewer can do.

Favor visible actions, interfaces, objects, steps, and decisions.

### 6. Resolution

Return to the central metaphor, question, or situation.

### 7. Aunt Truth close

Aunt Truth may return on screen to land the lesson and close the episode.

---

## Beat construction

Every production beat must define:

- narration
- visual subject
- visual action
- environment
- Aunt Truth visibility
- Maya visibility when relevant
- camera/framing intent
- motion intent
- on-screen text if needed
- sound effect intent
- music intent
- transition

Narration and visual description must be separate fields.

Never use the narration itself as the visual prompt.

---

## Visual hierarchy

Preferred visual priority:

1. The thing being discussed
2. The action being described
3. The consequence of the action
4. The viewer's experience
5. A metaphor that clarifies the concept
6. Aunt Truth on screen

This order is deliberate.

---

## Visual style

Maintain the established Truth Explains visual identity.

Scene art should normally use:

- mid-to-bright readable exposure
- warm or cool accent lighting as appropriate
- strong subject separation
- clear silhouettes
- grounded environments
- cinematic composition without excessive darkness

Daytime and normally lit indoor scenes should not look gloomy, thriller-like,
or underexposed.

Night and low-light scenes are allowed when narratively appropriate, but they
should remain readable.

Do not import dark social-card backgrounds into cinematic scene prompts merely
because those colors exist elsewhere in the brand system.

---

## Animation principle

Animation exists to communicate.

Movement should reveal:

- cause and effect
- attention
- risk
- sequence
- transformation
- comparison
- emotional reaction

Avoid motion added only because the tool can animate something.

Stillness is valid when it serves the beat.

---

## On-screen text

Use text sparingly.

Text should:

- reinforce a key phrase
- identify a concept
- show a message/interface
- make a practical instruction easier to remember

Do not duplicate full narration as subtitles baked into scene art.

Captions are handled separately.

---

## Music

Music supports the emotional rhythm of the explanation.

It must never compete with Aunt Truth's narration.

Use music to shape:

- curiosity
- tension
- realization
- reassurance
- momentum
- resolution

Narration remains the dominant audio element.

---

## Sound effects

SFX should make the visual world feel real and help clarify actions.

Examples:

- key turn
- lock click
- notification
- keyboard tap
- phone vibration
- door close
- message send
- subtle transition whoosh

Avoid overloading scenes with effects.

---

## Editing principle

The edit follows the narration's meaning, not arbitrary shot duration.

Cut when:

- the idea changes
- attention needs redirecting
- an action completes
- a metaphor progresses
- emotional emphasis changes

Do not hold Aunt Truth on screen simply to fill narration time.

---

## Episode success test

A finished Truth Explains episode should still make visual sense with the
narration muted.

The narration should still make conceptual sense without the visuals.

Together, they should be stronger than either one alone.

That is the production standard.
